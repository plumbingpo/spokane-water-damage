Hardwood Floor Water Damage: What Spokane Homeowners Should Know

It does not take long for water to change a wood floor. Boards take on moisture from below and from the edges, swell against each other and start to cup, with the edges rising higher than the center. A floor in this condition is not necessarily ruined. Solid hardwood that is dried in the first few days often flattens back out. What matters is removing moisture from the wood and from its subfloor steadily and under control.

Drying mats are how our team handles wet wood floors. Each mat is sealed against the floor and hooked to a vacuum unit, which pulls moisture upward through seams and boards as dehumidifiers dry the air in the room. This takes longer than drying carpet or drywall, often a week or more. Hold off on the sander until every board is fully dry. Sand a cupped floor too soon and it will crown later.

Signs to Look For

  • Sections of flooring that have risen, forming a tent shape along a seam
  • Flooring that gives under your weight in the area that got wet
  • Boards shrinking apart and leaving gaps following a rushed dry out
  • Board edges sitting higher than the centers, called cupping
  • Fasteners working loose, heard as squeaks where the floor was soaked
  • Baseboards or door trim swelling where the floor meets the wall
How We Fix It

What Happens After You Call

1

Measure the Moisture

We take readings on the boards, the subfloor and unaffected flooring nearby for comparison. That tells us how deep the water went and whether saving the floor is realistic.

2

Extract Standing Water

Rugs and furnishings come off the affected boards while pooled water is vacuumed up at once. Boards swell less when the seams stop taking in water sooner.

3

Set Drying Mats

A vacuum system hooked to mats sealed on the surface lifts moisture out of the boards from above. Dehumidifiers control the room so the wood dries evenly without cracking.

4

Monitor and Advise

Readings are logged every one to two days and the equipment stays until the wet zone equals the dry comparison areas. At that point you hear from us if it needs nothing, some replacement boards, or a refinish.

Why Speed Matters

The Damage Timeline

First hours

Seepage Below the Boards

Seams let the water through, and it fans out in the layer between flooring and subfloor. The surface may wipe dry while the underside stays soaked.

24 to 48 hours

Edges Begin to Rise

Swelling sets in and board edges lift. Finish may turn cloudy. Mold can begin on the subfloor and the unfinished underside of the wood.

Day 3 to 7

Boards Start Lifting

Swelling builds pressure across the floor. With fasteners losing their hold and boards compressing at the edges, some areas can lift away from the subfloor.

After a week

Beyond Drying

Expect a good chance of mold below, dark staining and boards that have buckled. At this stage most of the wet area needs to be replaced rather than dried.

Behind the Surface

The Damage You Do Not See

Stains From Nails and Staples

Staples and flooring nails corrode when moisture lingers, and their grip weakens. The rust also bleeds into the wood as dark stains that sanding may not remove.

Mold Under Boards

Between the boards and the subfloor there is only darkness and still air. You will smell it before you see it, because nothing shows on the surface.

Drying Too Fast

High heat or too much airflow pulls moisture from the surface faster than the core can follow. The result can be cracks, splits and permanent gaps between boards.

Wet Subfloor

Long after the boards seem dry, the subfloor may still be holding water. If it is not dried too, the floor will cup again or grow mold from underneath.

With cupping, moisture from underneath lifts each board's edges above its middle. Flip that and you have crowning, a high center that commonly results from sanding cupped boards early. The worst of the three is buckling. Flooring comes free of the subfloor and lifts, occasionally several inches. Many cupped floors recover. Those that buckle seldom survive.
When a small spill causes mild cupping, the wood occasionally settles as it dries out. A true leak is different, as trapped moisture beneath the boards holds the swelling in place unless something removes it. Drying the floor from above and the subfloor underneath gives the boards the best chance to settle flat again.
Shut off the water if possible, then soak up whatever is standing with towels or a mop. Take up rugs and shift furniture so nothing keeps moisture pressed to the wood. Keep heaters away from the floor and let the air conditioner or a dehumidifier do the work. Then have the moisture under the boards measured, since the surface dries long before the wood below.

Short surface exposure, cleaned up before edges swell, is generally fine. Replacement is needed once planks swell, delaminate or have moisture below them.
